# FLYER Visual Direction System

Use these ten controls to convert a vague idea into visible, shootable decisions. Do not output all analysis unless it helps the user; use it to make the prompts precise.

## 1. Story Beat

Define what visibly changes during the clip.

- Write a one-sentence event with a start state, turning point, and settling state.
- Give the shot one dominant emotion and one secondary tension.
- Replace abstractions with evidence. “Lonely” becomes an unanswered glance, an empty chair, restrained breathing, or a hand that stops before touching a door.

Cheap failure: a beautiful person standing still while effects happen around them.

## 2. Character Performance

Direct the face and body as a continuous performance.

- Specify gaze target, eyelid tension, brow activity, jaw or lip tension, breath, and the moment the expression changes.
- Specify body weight, balance, shoulder tension, hand purpose, and contact with props or surfaces.
- Use small readable changes. A held breath, delayed blink, or fingers loosening can carry more emotion than an exaggerated reaction.

Cheap failure: blank glamour face, symmetrical fashion pose, weightless limbs, or hands that touch nothing.

## 3. Blocking and Spatial Relations

Explain where bodies and objects are and why.

- Define foreground, subject plane, background, entrances, exits, obstacles, and distances.
- Preserve screen direction and eyelines.
- Let movement have a route and a reason. Note which foot initiates, where the body stops, and what object is crossed or touched when useful.

Cheap failure: subjects sliding through the scene without floor contact or changing sides between cuts.

## 4. Shot Size and Camera Position

Choose framing from story information.

- Wide shots reveal isolation, geography, scale, or approach.
- Medium shots reveal blocking, hands, and relationships.
- Close-ups reveal a decision, not merely attractiveness.
- State camera height and angle: eye level, waist height, floor level, overhead, or shoulder height.
- Use low or high angles only when the power or vulnerability effect serves the beat.

Cheap failure: extreme low angle, dutch angle, or ultra-wide distortion added only to look “cinematic.”

## 5. Composition and Depth

Build a readable vertical frame.

- Protect the face, hands, and important props from interface and subtitle zones.
- Use negative space to indicate direction, absence, threat, or expectation.
- Separate foreground, middle ground, and background with occlusion, scale, haze, or focus.
- Decide whether the frame is stable, slightly off-balance, or deliberately compressed.
- Keep one dominant visual hierarchy.

Cheap failure: centered subject plus random symmetrical background, every object equally sharp, or empty space with no narrative job.

## 6. Lens Feel and Camera Motion

Describe perspective behavior rather than worshipping hardware.

- Use “wide environmental perspective,” “natural perspective,” “compressed portrait perspective,” or an approximate focal-length feel when helpful.
- Choose one main camera behavior per beat: locked, slow push, lateral track, restrained handheld drift, orbit, crane, or subject-led follow.
- State what motivates the motion and where it settles.
- Keep acceleration and deceleration physical.

Cheap failure: simultaneous orbit, zoom, crane, whip pan, and shake; motion with no subject relationship.

## 7. Lighting Logic

Name the source, direction, quality, contrast, and exposure behavior.

- Source: window, overcast sky, streetlamp, fire, fluorescent ceiling, moonlit sky, phone screen, reflected snow, or motivated practical.
- Direction: front, three-quarter, side, back, top, or bounced fill.
- Quality: hard, soft, broken, diffused, humid, dusty, or reflected.
- Contrast: describe shadow density, fill level, highlight rolloff, and whether the eyes retain catchlight.
- Keep skin and background exposure logically related.

Cheap failure: unrelated rim lights, neon on every surface, evenly bright faces at night, clipped highlights, or shadows that point in several directions.

## 8. Materials and Atmosphere

Give surfaces different physical responses.

- Skin: pores, fine facial hair, moisture, dryness, fatigue, blemishes, or makeup appropriate to the story.
- Fabric: weave, weight, drape, seams, compression, wrinkles, wetness, dust, wear, and edge behavior.
- Hard surfaces: scratches, oxidation, fingerprints, condensation, chipped paint, polished reflections, or rough scattering.
- Atmosphere: rain, dust, smoke, mist, breath vapor, insects, or suspended particles only when motivated.

Cheap failure: plastic skin, spotless costumes in harsh environments, identical gloss on every object, or decorative particles with no source.

## 9. Color and Image Texture

Build a limited, motivated palette.

- Choose one dominant family, one support family, and at most one accent.
- Tie color to sources, production design, time of day, or emotional transition.
- Define black level, highlight warmth, saturation restraint, grain, halation, bloom, and edge softness only as needed.
- Preserve skin-tone credibility unless stylization intentionally changes it.

Cheap failure: teal-orange applied everywhere, crushed blacks hiding anatomy, oversaturated neon, or heavy grain used to cover weak detail.

## 10. Time, Motion, and Sound

Make the clip a temporal event.

- Divide time into meaningful beats rather than equal slices.
- Give every action an entry, readable peak, and settling state.
- Specify ambience, foley, breath, dialogue, and music separately.
- Quote exact dialogue. Keep lip-sync lines short enough for the shot.
- Let silence or room tone carry tension when appropriate.

Cheap failure: action begins at the last second, constant music swell, every sound at equal volume, or dialogue that competes with the visual beat.

## Coherence Test

Ask five questions:

1. Does the camera position reveal the most important information at the right moment?
2. Does the lighting source exist in the scene?
3. Do materials, weather, and wardrobe show the same physical conditions?
4. Do face, gaze, body weight, and hand action express the same emotion?
5. Could removing an effect make the scene stronger? If yes, remove it.
